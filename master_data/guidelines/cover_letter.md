## Core Principle: Personal Email, Not Application Document

The cover letter is not a summary of your resume. It is a direct message to a specific human who is hiring for a specific problem. Every sentence should answer: "Why should this person keep reading?"

Write like you're sending a Slack message to someone you respect — clear, direct, no corporate filler.

## Three-Paragraph Structure (follow exactly)

### Paragraph 1 — HOOK (2–4 sentences)

The first sentence is everything. It should make the reader think "huh, this person actually thought about our problem" — not "another candidate applying to every posting."

**Strong hooks:**
- Reference a specific problem the role exists to solve (inferred from the JD or the company's known domain)
- Connect the company's space to a concrete moment from your own experience
- Ask a rhetorical question that the story paragraph will answer

**Weak hooks (never use):**
- "I am writing to express my interest in..."
- "I've been following your company for years..."
- Opening with your own name, school, or GPA
- "I believe I would be a great fit because..."

**When the JD is sparse** (just a title and company name): Don't fake specifics. Acknowledge what you know about the company's domain and connect it directly to the story you're about to tell. Honest inference beats fabricated JD references.

### Paragraph 2 — STORY (4–7 sentences)

Tell one story. The whole paragraph is that story. No intro like "One project that comes to mind is..." — just start from the context.

Structure within the paragraph:
1. Problem/situation (1 sentence) — what was the constraint or pain?
2. What you built or did (2–3 sentences) — specific, technical, yours
3. The hard part (1 sentence, optional) — what made it non-trivial?
4. The outcome (1 sentence) — exact metric, adoption number, or measurable result

**Don't paraphrase.** "I built a chatbot that improved response time" is a paraphrase. "I indexed 5 years of team emails into Azure AI Search — ~50K documents — so the on-call engineer could get step-by-step diagnosis instead of hunting through Confluence at 2am" is a story.

### Paragraph 3 — CONNECTOR + CLOSE (2–3 sentences)

Draw one explicit line:
- What skill or judgment did the story demonstrate?
- What thing from the role does it map to exactly?

Then one short close: express interest in a conversation. Nothing more.

**No recap of your resume.** No "In addition to the above, I also have experience in..." No "I believe I would bring tremendous value." Just the connection and the ask.

## Absolute Rules

- **No em dashes (—)** — use commas or semicolons instead
- **No** "passionate," "thrilled," "excited to apply," "highly motivated"
- **No** prose skill lists: "I have experience in Python, Docker, and Kubernetes"
- **No** "I am the perfect candidate" or "ideal fit"
- **No** paragraph openers: "Furthermore," "Moreover," "In addition," "Additionally"
- **No** bullet points or markdown — plain text only
- **No** sign-off — the document builder adds that
- Contractions are fine (I've, it's, didn't) — they sound human

## Length and Tone Calibration

- **Target:** 180–280 words total
- **Startup/early-stage:** 160–220 words, punchier and more direct
- **Enterprise/big tech:** 220–300 words, slightly more textured — but still no padding

One paragraph per idea. White space is good. Long paragraphs signal rambling.

## What Makes a Cover Letter Memorable

The best cover letters do one thing: they make the reader curious about the candidate before they look at the resume. That happens when:
- The hook names a real problem, not a platitude
- The story has a metric or outcome that sounds real (because it is)
- The connector is specific enough to show the candidate read the JD

The worst cover letters do: generic enthusiasm + resume summary + "thank you for your consideration." These go straight to the archive.
